Lesson 05

Learning how to learn

TL;DR

Breadth is only affordable if you learn efficiently, and most people learn with methods that feel productive but barely work: re-reading, highlighting, cramming. The evidence-backed engine is small. Retrieval practice means testing yourself instead of re-reading. Spacing means revisiting over expanding intervals. Interleaving means mixing topics. The Feynman technique means explaining it simply, which exposes the gap. Deliberate practice means working the specific edge of your ability with feedback. The unifying principle: effortful, slightly uncomfortable learning sticks; easy, fluent review doesn't. This is the metabolic engine for both the latticework and polymathy.

🎯 For the builder

Why it matters — Learning how to learn is the one skill that compounds into every other one. If your acquisition rate is 2× better, every field you ever pick up is 2× cheaper, for the rest of your life.

What it changes in your decisions — Stop spending time on comfortable methods that feel like learning (highlighting, re-watching). Reallocate it to the uncomfortable ones that actually encode (testing, explaining, spacing).

Ask yourself — "Am I doing the thing that feels like progress, or the thing that produces it?"

Risk if ignored — You confuse familiarity with knowledge. The material feels easy because you've seen it, and you discover at the worst moment that you can't actually retrieve or use it.

A latticework you can actually deploy

Memory that fires when you need it — not memory that feels good while you're reviewing.

The central trap in learning is the fluency illusion. When material is in front of you and feels easy, your brain reads "easy to process" as "I know this." You don't. Recognition is not recall. Re-reading a chapter until it feels smooth builds familiarity with the text, not the ability to produce the idea when the text is gone. Nearly every weak study method shares this flaw. It optimizes for in-the-moment ease, which is precisely the wrong signal.

The counterintuitive corollary is well-established in cognitive science (Bjork's desirable difficulties): the conditions that make learning feel harder and slower in the moment usually make it stronger and more durable. Struggling to retrieve, spacing things out far enough that you've half-forgotten, mixing problem types so you can't autopilot — all of these feel worse and work better. If your studying is comfortable, be suspicious.

The high-leverage techniques

Retrieval practice — test, don't review

Trying to pull information out of memory strengthens it far more than putting it back in by re-reading. This is the testing effect, one of the most replicated findings in the field. Practically: close the book and write what you remember. Use flashcards that force production. Answer questions before checking. The effort of retrieval is the mechanism — which is why looking at the answer too soon destroys the benefit.

Spacing — revisit over expanding intervals

Cramming loads material into short-term memory, where it decays within days. Spaced repetition means reviewing at growing intervals — a day, then three, then a week, then a month. It interrupts forgetting right at the edge, and each effortful recall resets the decay clock further out. The same total study time, spaced, produces dramatically more retention than massed. Tools like Anki automate the scheduling, but the principle works on paper.

Interleaving — mix it up

Studying one topic to exhaustion before the next (blocking) feels orderly and performs worse than interleaving: mixing related topics or problem types in a session. Interleaving forces your brain to choose the right approach each time instead of running on autopilot — exactly the skill you need when problems arrive unlabeled in real life.

The Feynman technique — explain it to a child

Named for Richard Feynman's habit of reducing any concept to a plain explanation. The loop:

  1. Pick a concept and explain it, out loud or written, in simple language — as if to a smart twelve-year-old.
  2. Watch for the spots where you reach for jargon, hand-wave, or stall. That stall is the exact location of your ignorance.
  3. Go back to the source for only that gap, then re-explain.
  4. Repeat until the explanation is simple and gap-free.

It's diabolically effective because jargon hides holes from yourself, and plain language can't. The technique doubles as retrieval practice (you're producing, not reviewing) and is the fastest way to reach the "working fluency" bar that polymathy needs. Teaching a thing is how you find out you don't yet know it.

Deliberate practice — work the edge, with feedback

From Anders Ericsson's research on expertise: improvement doesn't come from doing an activity, where you plateau at "good enough." It comes from deliberate practice: targeting the specific sub-skill just beyond your current ability, with tight feedback, repeatedly. The ingredients are a well-defined stretch goal, full focus, immediate feedback, and relentless work on weaknesses rather than replaying strengths. Ten years of casual driving makes no one an expert driver. It's the structure of the practice, not the hours, that builds skill.

A concrete weekly loop for picking up a new field: read a little → immediately close it and explain it (Feynman + retrieval) → note the gaps → mix today's topic with last week's (interleaving) → revisit on an expanding schedule (spacing). It feels slower than binge-reading. It is several times more durable.

How this powers the rest of the module

This lesson is the engine under the other five. The latticework demands you learn many models to fluency, not familiarity — that's retrieval, spacing, and Feynman. Polymathy demands you reach working competence in field after field affordably — that's the entire economic case for learning efficiently. And first-principles reasoning is itself a learnable skill that improves with deliberate practice on real problems. Learn to learn, and everything else in this module gets cheaper.

📦 Mini-case — the two study groups. The repeated lab finding, from Roediger & Karpicke's testing-effect studies: students who re-read a passage four times feel more confident and score worse a week later than students who read once and were tested three times. Fluency and learning move in opposite directions. Re-reading maximizes the feeling; retrieval maximizes the retention. Every technique in this lesson is that finding, generalized: if studying feels smooth, it probably isn't working.

Failure modes

  • Mistaking familiarity for mastery — The fluency illusion, the headline trap.
  • Passive review — Re-reading and highlighting feel productive and barely move retention.
  • Massed cramming — Works for the exam tomorrow, gone by next week. Useless for building a durable lattice.
  • Practice without feedback — Something gets repeated the same way forever, reinforcing flaws instead of fixing them.
  • Avoiding the discomfort — Quitting a method because it feels hard, which is exactly the signal it's working.

Practitioner checklist

  • Am I testing myself (retrieval) more than I'm re-reading?
  • Are my reviews spaced over expanding intervals rather than massed?
  • Can I explain the idea in plain language without reaching for jargon?
  • Is my practice aimed at the edge of my ability, with real feedback?
  • Am I treating discomfort as a signal of learning, not a reason to stop?
