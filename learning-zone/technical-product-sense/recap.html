Technical product sense — recap & real-world examples

Real-world examples & war stories

The duplicate-charge outage. A payment request times out. The client retries. Without idempotency, the customer gets charged twice. One missing idempotency key turns into a support-and-refund fire drill. 🎯 Takeaway: "what happens on retry?" is one of the highest-leverage questions a PM can ask. It stays invisible until the day it isn't.

The p95 that averaged fine. A feature ships with a healthy 200 ms average response. But the team's most valuable power users — the ones with the most data — quietly suffer 4-second loads. Nobody checked the p95 tail. 🎯 Takeaway: averages hide your worst experiences. Always ask for percentiles.

The migration that wasn't scoped. A "quick" feature turns out to need a relationship the data model never stored. A two-week estimate becomes a two-month migration. 🎯 Takeaway: "does this data exist, in a usable shape?" belongs in discovery, not mid-build.

Air Canada's chatbot invented a refund policy (2024). A support bot gave a confident, well-formed, and wrong answer. A tribunal held the airline to it. 🎯 Takeaway: the AI-specific failure is a 200-OK wrong answer. That is why validation, grounding, and a wrong-answer path are core, not polish.

The AI feature nobody could debug. A model feature ships without evals or observability. Weeks later, quality has drifted and no one can tell why — the prompt, the retrieval, the model version, or the data could all be the cause. 🎯 Takeaway: evals and observability are the reliability of an AI feature. The debt of skipping them comes due exactly when you can least afford it.

The through-line: technical product sense is the ability to see the system behind the feature. That means the boxes a request travels, the contracts between them, where the data lives, where time and money go, how it fails, and what it costs to change. For the AI PM, every one of these gains a probabilistic twist. The discipline stays the same: understand the shape of the system well enough to build with it, and to earn the trust of the engineers who do.

Walk-away question: "For this feature, can I draw the system on a whiteboard — the boxes, the slow hop, the failure paths — and if there's a model in it, is the product the trustworthy machinery around the model, not the model itself?"

Test yourself

  1. "It's slow" and "it falls over under load" — why must you never conflate them?

    AnswerLatency (one request's time) and scale (how many requests) need different fixes. Latency needs you to find and cache the slow hop. Scale needs horizontal scaling and relief for the shared bottleneck. Fixing the wrong one wastes months. (Latency, scale & performance)

  2. A payment call times out and the app retries. What property makes that safe, and how does it work?

    AnswerIdempotency. The client sends a unique key per attempt. The server returns the original result for a repeated key, so a retry can't double-charge. (APIs & contracts)

  3. Why is "where does this data live, and how is it shaped?" a product question?

    AnswerThe data model decides what features are possible without a migration. A question the model can't answer is a feature you can't ship. Analytical reads also don't belong on the transactional store. (Data & the data model)

  4. Name the four tools that keep a dependency slowdown from becoming your outage.

    AnswerTimeouts cap the wait. Retries with backoff and jitter stop the storm from synchronizing. Circuit breakers fail fast while a dependency is sick. And a designed fallback controls what the user sees. (Reliability & failure)

  5. What's the blast-radius question, and when do you ask it?

    Answer"If this piece were compromised tonight, what does the attacker hold tomorrow?" Ask it at design time, for every component, credential, and data store — not after the incident. (Security & privacy sense)
